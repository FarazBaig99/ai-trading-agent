## 0. Core Philosophy (read this first)

- **Candles are a tool, not a system.** They are very good at giving *early reversal signals*. They give **no price targets**, so targets have to come from Western tools (Ch. 16).
- **Context beats pattern.** Where a pattern appears (the trend before it, nearby support/resistance, overbought/oversold, the major trend) matters more than how perfect it looks.
  - A bottom reversal needs a prior decline, and a top reversal needs a prior rally. The same two-candle shape inside a range or against the trend is *not* the pattern.
  - A bullish signal right at resistance (or a bearish one at support) is a poor trade: "you'd be buying at resistance."
- **"Reversal" really means "trend change."** After a top signal the market may go sideways rather than fall. If the trend changes from up to neutral, the signal worked. Nison's analogy: brake lights tell you the car is stopping, not that it will reverse.
- **Trade reversal signals only in the direction of the major trend.** A top pattern in a bull market is a reason to *exit longs*, not to go short. Then wait for a bullish signal on the pullback and buy.
- **Use the close.** Patterns are confirmed on the session close. "Surpassed" or "broken" means *a close* beyond the level, not an intraday poke. Intraday penetrations that fail to close beyond a level leave it intact.
- **Risk/reward before every trade.** Many patterns finish only after the market has already moved a long way (long hammer shadow, tall third candle of a star, three soldiers/crows, engulfing with a huge real body). In that case, wait for a pullback toward the pattern's support or resistance to enter closer to your stop.
- **Every trade needs a price that proves you wrong** (stop level), usually the extreme of the pattern, on a close.
- **Subjectivity is normal.** Real patterns rarely match the textbook drawings. Judge a non-ideal pattern by (1) *how* it formed and (2) *where* it formed. Learn which patterns work in your own market.
- **Real body = "essence of the price move."** A long white body means bulls are in control and a long black body means bears are. Shrinking real bodies mean momentum is fading.
- **Shadows matter.** Long upper shadows ("bearish shadows") show rejection of higher prices. Long lower shadows ("bullish shadows") show rejection of lower prices. Several of them at the same level reinforce resistance or support.
- **Open and close are the emotionally charged points.** "Morning attack" is a big order at the open meant to move price, and "night attack" is the same at the close.
- Candles work on any timeframe (intraday, daily, weekly) and in any market (stocks, futures, FX, indexes).

## 1. Constructing Candles (Ch. 3)

- **Real body** is the range between open and close.
  - **White/hollow:** close > open.
  - **Black/filled:** close < open.
- **Upper shadow:** the session high above the body. **Lower shadow:** the session low below the body.
- **Shaven head:** no upper shadow. **Shaven bottom:** no lower shadow.
- **Spinning top:** a small real body of either color. The shadow length doesn't matter. It shows the prior momentum is weakening and is a component of stars, harami, and others. One spinning top is only a tentative clue.
- **Doji:** open equals close, or nearly so. It shows indecision and is a potential reversal (Ch. 8).
- **High-wave candle:** very long upper *and* lower shadows with a small body. The market "has lost its sense of direction." If the body is a doji, it is a long-legged doji.
- **Candles vs. bars:** candles use the same OHLC data, so every bar-chart tool still applies, plus candle-only signals. A bar chart showing higher highs, higher lows, and higher closes can look healthy while candles show small bodies and long upper shadows ("rising in agony").
- **FX, many indexes, and intraday charts:** open ≈ prior close, so bodies often touch. Be more flexible with gap requirements, such as gaps in stars or the open/close relationship in engulfing patterns.

## 2. Single-Candle Signals

### 2.1 Umbrella lines: Hammer and Hanging Man (Ch. 4)
Same shape, but the meaning depends on the prior trend.

**Shape rules (both):**
1. Small real body at the **upper end** of the range. Color is not important.
2. **Lower shadow at least 2x the body height**, ideally 2-3x.
3. **No or very short upper shadow.**
- The longer the lower shadow, the shorter the upper shadow, and the smaller the body, the more meaningful the signal.

**Hammer (bullish, "takuri" = feeling for the bottom):**
- Must follow a decline. Even a short-term decline is enough.
- **Confirmation not required.** A white hammer ("power line") is slightly more bullish, but color doesn't affect success much.
- The hammer's low becomes **support**. If the market closes under that low, the bullish view is voided.
- **Risk tip (Trader A vs. Trader B):** buying at the hammer's close puts the stop far away, under the low. Waiting for a dip into the lower shadow gives a tighter stop and better reward-to-risk.
- A hammer is also valuable when it *confirms* existing support (a prior low, trendline, or moving average).
- A **high-volume hammer** is less likely to retrace to its low, so it justifies buying more aggressively on the close.

**Hanging Man (bearish):**
- Must follow a rally, ideally an extended one, preferably at an all-time high or a high for a significant move.
- **Needs bearish confirmation:** at minimum the next session opens below its real body. Nison prefers a **close below the hanging man's real body**.
- Logic: buyers on the hanging-man open or close are left "hanging" with losses at new highs, so they may sell.
- If the next closes are higher (new high closes), the bull trend remains intact. Ignore unconfirmed hanging men.

### 2.2 Shooting Star and Inverted Hammer (Ch. 5)
Same shape again, with the meaning set by the trend.

**Shape:** small real body at the **lower end** of the range, **long upper shadow**, little or no lower shadow. Color is not important.

**Shooting Star (bearish):**
- Must come after a rally. Ideally its body gaps above the prior body, though the gap is not required. With no rising window, Nison is actually *more* comfortable that the trend is weakening, because a rising window is itself bullish.
- It is a single session, so it is usually **not a major reversal** and not pivotal resistance like an engulfing pattern or evening star. Several shooting stars at the same price greatly strengthen resistance.
- A close above the shooting star highs negates it.
- The gravestone doji is the extreme version (doji body), so it is more bearish.

**Inverted Hammer (bullish):**
- Comes after a decline.
- **Needs bullish confirmation:** the next session opens, and especially closes, above its real body. The line itself looks bearish because of the long upper shadow.
- Logic: shorts who sold on that session are now losing money, and short covering can start a rally.

### 2.3 Belt-Hold Lines (Ch. 6) ("yorikiri," a sumo push-out)
- **Bullish belt-hold (white opening shaven bottom):** a tall white candle that opens at or near its low and closes at or near the high. At a low price area it forecasts a rally.
- **Bearish belt-hold (black opening shaven head):** a tall black candle that opens at or near its high and closes near the low. At high prices it is a top signal.
- The longer the candle, the more significant it is. It is more important if it confirms resistance or support, or if no belt-holds have appeared for a while.
- **Negation:** a close above a bearish belt-hold means the uptrend resumes. A close below a bullish belt-hold means selling resumes.

### 2.4 Doji family (Ch. 8, "The Magic Doji")
- **Definition:** open equals close. A few ticks' difference can still count. Treat a near-doji as a doji when:
  1. it stands out among tall candles (not among many small bodies),
  2. it appears at an important market junction,
  3. the market is extremely overbought or oversold, or
  4. other technical signals are alerting at the same time.
  - It is better to heed a false warning than to ignore a real one.
- **A doji's significance increases if:** later candles confirm it, the market is overbought or oversold, and the chart doesn't already have many doji.
- **Northern doji (in a rally):**
  - Strong at calling tops, especially **a doji after a tall white candle** (the market is "tired").
  - **Technique:** take the **highest high of the tall white candle and the doji** as resistance, on a close. A close above it means the market is "refreshed" and bullish.
  - A doji alone changes the short-term trend only from **up to up/neutral**, not to down. If it confirms other resistance, it goes to neutral or down.
  - Actions: lighten longs, raise stops, sell calls.
  - If the doji is also a **new high close**, wait for bearish confirmation (a close under the doji's close).
- **Southern doji (in a decline):** much less reliable for bottoms, because markets can "fall of their own weight." It needs more confirmation. Take it seriously only if it confirms support, or has high volume, etc.
- **Doji in a box (trading range):** no forecasting value because there is no trend to reverse. The exception is a doji at the top or bottom edge of the range, which confirms resistance or support.
- **Long-legged doji (rickshaw man):** a doji with very long upper and lower shadows. The market is confused and separating from its trend.
- **Gravestone doji:** open, low, and close at the session low, with a long upper shadow. It is the "ultimate shooting star," strongest at tops ("the gravestone of bulls who died defending their territory"). It increases the odds of a turn but **does not forecast the size of the move**.
- **Dragonfly doji:** open, high, and close at the session high, with a long lower shadow. It is the bullish counterpart, like a hammer with no body.

## 3. Two-Candle Patterns

### 3.1 Engulfing Patterns (Ch. 4): major reversal signals
**Three criteria:**
1. A clearly definable prior trend (an uptrend for bearish, a downtrend for bullish), even if short-term.
2. The **second real body engulfs the first real body**. The shadows don't need to be engulfed.
3. The second body is the **opposite color**. Exception: the first candle may be a doji, and a doji engulfed by a very large body is still valid.

- **Bullish engulfing ("hugging line"):** in a downtrend, a white body wraps around the prior black body. Buying overwhelms selling.
- **Bearish engulfing:** in an uptrend, a black body wraps around the prior white body. Supply overwhelms demand.
- **Reliability boosters:**
  1. The first body is very small (a spinning top) and the second is very long.
  2. The pattern comes after a protracted or very fast move (overextended market).
  3. Heavy volume on the second candle, ideally with lighter volume on the first.
  4. The second body engulfs *several* prior bodies.
- **Support/resistance use:**
  - The **lowest low** of the two candles in a bullish engulfing pattern is support.
  - The **highest high** of a bearish engulfing pattern is resistance, on a close.
  - Use these levels to enter on a pullback when the pattern completed far from the extreme.
- **Versus the Western outside reversal day:** an outside reversal needs a new high or low. A candle engulfing pattern only needs the real bodies to wrap. So candles can give a signal where the bar chart gives none (a timing advantage).
- A close above a bearish engulfing pattern's high is a bullish breakout. Momentum traders can buy it. Wait for a close, not an intraday break.

### 3.2 Dark-Cloud Cover (Ch. 4): bearish
- **Context:** an uptrend, or the top of a congestion band.
- **Candle 1:** a strong white body.
- **Candle 2:** opens **above the prior session's high**. Nison chose this stricter rule; a variant opens above the prior close. It then closes **deep into the white body, ideally more than 50%**.
- If it closes under the midpoint of the white body, it is more reliable. If it doesn't, wait for further bearish confirmation.
- **Strengthening factors:**
  1. Deeper penetration. Full coverage would be a bearish engulfing pattern ("total eclipse" vs. the dark-cloud's "partial eclipse").
  2. In a long rally, a shaven-head/shaven-bottom white candle followed by a black candle that opens on its high and closes on its low.
  3. The black candle opens above *major resistance* and then fails.
  4. Very heavy volume on the second day's open (a buying blow-off, "the ship is the Titanic"). In futures, high open interest is also a warning.
- **Resistance:** the highest high of the two sessions. Shorts can place a stop at the new high.
- A long white candle closing above the dark-cloud or engulfing high suggests a new rally.

### 3.3 Piercing Pattern (Ch. 4): bullish
- **Context:** a downtrend.
- **Candle 1:** a long black body.
- **Candle 2:** opens lower, ideally **below the prior low**. It closes **more than halfway into the black body**. This rule is *stricter* than for the dark-cloud cover.
- Same strengthening factors as the dark-cloud cover, reversed. The lowest low is support.
- **Weaker relatives (same shape, less penetration):**
  - **On-neck:** a small white candle that closes near the prior *low*. Bearish continuation.
  - **In-neck:** a small white candle that closes slightly into the prior body. Bearish continuation.
  - **Thrusting:** a longer white candle that closes into the body but under the midpoint. Bearish in a downtrend, though two thrusting lines within a few days, or one at established support, can be bullish. Generally wait for a higher close the next session.
  - The key idea: **more than 50% penetration** is needed for a potent bottom.

### 3.4 Harami and Harami Cross (Ch. 6)
- **Harami ("pregnant"):** an unusually long real body (the "mother") followed by a small real body (the "baby") contained **within the first real body**. Shadows may extend outside it.
  - Colors are usually opposite, but that is not required.
  - The market is "losing its breath." It is usually a weaker reversal than engulfing patterns. It often turns the trend **neutral** rather than reversing it.
- **Versus the Western inside day:** an inside day needs the whole high-low range inside the prior range. A harami needs only the body inside the body.
- **Harami cross (the "petrifying pattern"):** the second candle is a doji.
  - It is more potent than a regular harami. The smaller the second body, the stronger the signal.
  - It works better at tops. "A long trader ignores a harami cross after a very long white candle at their peril."
- **Use:** scale back longs and tighten stops, especially when it appears at a resistance line or other Western resistance.

### 3.5 Tweezers Tops and Bottoms (Ch. 6)
- Two or more candles with **matching highs** (tweezers top, in a rally) or **matching lows** (tweezers bottom, in a decline). They can be formed by bodies, shadows, or doji.
- **Ideal form:** a long first candle and a small second body.
- They are only minor signals on daily or intraday charts. **They matter when the same two candles also form another pattern:**
  - harami cross + tweezers top
  - hanging man + tweezers top
  - shooting star + tweezers top
  - dark-cloud cover (variation) + tweezers top
  - hammer + tweezers bottom
  - piercing pattern (variation) + tweezers bottom
- On **weekly or monthly** charts, consecutive tweezers can be important on their own.
- They are negated by a close beyond the matching highs or lows. A tweezers top in a market that isn't overbought is less meaningful.

### 3.6 Counterattack Lines (Ch. 6)
- Opposite-colored candles with the **same close**.
- **Bullish counterattack:** in a decline, a long black candle is followed by a session that opens sharply lower and rallies to close at about the prior close. It is weaker than a piercing pattern (no penetration of the body) but still shows a change in direction.
- **Bearish counterattack:** in a rally, a long white candle is followed by a session that opens sharply higher (ideally above the prior high) and falls back to the prior close. It is weaker than a dark-cloud cover.
- **Key requirement:** a strong opening move in the trend direction, then a full retreat to unchanged. A close that isn't exactly equal is acceptable.
- It can give a one-session early warning before slower patterns like three black crows.

### 3.7 Separating Lines (Ch. 7): continuation
- **Same open** as the previous opposite-colored candle.
- **Bullish:** in a rally, after a black candle the market gaps up to open at the black candle's open and closes higher (white). Ideally the white candle is a bullish belt-hold.
- **Bearish:** in a decline, after a white candle the market opens at the white candle's open and closes lower.
- They are rare. A bullish separating line that forms right at resistance can fail, so wait for a close above resistance.

### 3.8 Star / Doji Star (basic two-line component, Ch. 5)
- **Star:** a small real body that **gaps away from the prior large real body**. The bodies must not overlap, though the shadows may. In a downtrend it is also called a "raindrop."
- **Doji star:** the star is a doji. It warns that the trend may be ending, and is a stronger warning than a regular star.
- The star shows a stalemate after one side had been in control.

## 4. Three-Candle (and Multi-Candle) Patterns

### 4.1 Morning Star (bullish, Ch. 5)
- Candle 1: an **extended black** body (bears in command).
- Candle 2: a **small body (star)** that doesn't touch body 1 (selling force is fading).
- Candle 3: a **white body that pushes deeply into candle 1's black body** (bulls have seized control).
- **Context:** must follow a decline.
- Ideally there is a gap between bodies 2 and 3. In Nison's experience, **a lack of this gap or an overlap does not weaken the pattern**. The decisive factors are a spinning-top middle and deep penetration by candle 3.
- **Support** is the lowest low of the three candles.
- Because the third candle is often tall, the signal can be late. Wait for a pullback toward the low for a better reward-to-risk.
- Flexibility for FX, indexes, and intraday charts: bodies can touch.

### 4.2 Evening Star (bearish)
- A long white body, then a star (white or black) gapping above it, then a **black body closing sharply into the first white body**.
- **Context:** after an uptrend.
- Traffic-light analogy: green, then yellow, then red.
- **Resistance** is the highest high of the three candles.
- **Factors increasing reliability (both stars):**
  1. No overlap among the three real bodies.
  2. The third candle closes deeply into the first body.
  3. Light volume on candle 1 and heavy volume on candle 3.
- **Versus the Western island top:** an island needs the *shadows* of the middle session to clear sessions 1 and 3. An evening star needs only the **real body** of session 2 to be above bodies 1 and 3, so it gives more signals.
- Full name: the "three-river" morning/evening star, after Nobunaga's battle crossing three rivers. A close above the evening star's resistance (or below the morning star's support) means the other side has won.

### 4.3 Morning Doji Star / Evening Doji Star
- The middle candle is a doji. The evening doji star is a distinctive and meaningful top, and the morning doji star can be a meaningful bottom.

### 4.4 Abandoned Baby (top and bottom)
- A doji star whose **shadows gap** from both the prior and following candles: a gap before and a gap after.
- It is the same as a Western **island reversal where the island is a doji**.
- It is **very rare** and very strong. A tiny real body in place of the doji can be accepted as a variation.

### 4.5 Upside-Gap Two Crows (bearish, very rare)
- In an uptrend:
  1. a long white candle,
  2. a small black body that **gaps above** the white body (but closes above the white close), then
  3. a second black body that **opens above the first black's open and closes below the first black's close**.
- New highs fail twice and the second close is lower. If prices don't regain high ground on the 4th session, expect lower prices.
- A prior rising window makes it less bearish.

### 4.6 Three Black Crows (bearish)
- **Three declining consecutive long black candles** at high prices or after a mature advance.
- Each closes at or near its low, and ideally each opens within the prior body. Openings *below* the prior close can be even more bearish.
- "Bad news has wings."
- Since it completes late, it is most useful for longer-term traders. Look for earlier clues, such as a failed new high on the first crow or a bearish counterattack.

### 4.7 Three (Advancing) White Soldiers (bullish)
- **Three long white candles with consecutively higher closes.** Each opens within or near the prior white body and closes at or near its high.
- Bullish at a low price area or after a period of stable prices. Be cautious if the candles are very extended (overbought).
- It completes far from the lows, so reward-to-risk can be poor. On corrections, **the 1st or 2nd soldier often acts as support**.
- **Advance block:** the 2nd and 3rd candles (or just the 3rd) weaken, with progressively smaller bodies or long upper shadows. The rally is in trouble, so protect longs. It is especially relevant in mature uptrends.
- **Stalled (deliberation) pattern:** two long white candles followed by a small white body, either gapping up (a star) or "riding on the shoulder" of the prior body. Bulls are temporarily exhausted.
- Advance block and stalled patterns are for **liquidating or protecting longs, not shorting**. They matter more at high price levels.

### 4.8 Tri-Star (very rare)
- **Three doji**, with the middle one gapping above (top) or below (bottom) the others, shaped like a star pattern or a mini head-and-shoulders.
- From a single Japanese source, but used for decades. It greatly increases reversal odds but not the size of the move.

### 4.9 Rising / Falling Three Methods (continuation, Ch. 7)
- **Rising three methods:**
  1. A long white candle.
  2. About 3 small bodies (2-5 acceptable) drift lower or sideways, staying **within the white candle's high-low range** (shadows included). Black is ideal, but any color works.
  3. A strong white candle opens above the prior close and **closes above candle 1's close**.
- **Falling three methods:** the mirror image. A long black candle, small rising bodies within its range, then a black candle closing below candle 1's close.
- They resemble Western **bull/bear flags and pennants**. The pattern is a "rest from battle."
- **Volume ideal:** the highest volume on the two long candles and declining volume on the small ones.
- **Risk:** the stop is under the first white candle's low, and completion may be far from that low. A rising three methods that completes **at resistance** is not a good buy, so wait for a close above resistance.
- They can also reinforce a support area at a turn.

## 5. Windows (Gaps) and Window-Based Patterns (Ch. 7)

- **Window = gap.** The Japanese say "closing the window" where the West says "filling the gap."
  - A **true window requires no overlap of the shadows**: a space between the prior high and the current low (rising) or the prior low and the current high (falling). A gap between real bodies only is *not* a window.
- **Rising window:** bullish continuation. **Falling window:** bearish continuation.
- **"Go in the direction of the window":** buy dips after a rising window and sell bounces after a falling window.
- **"Corrections stop at the window":** the **entire window** is a support or resistance zone.
  - The **critical level is the bottom of a rising window** (or the top of a falling one), *on a close*. A close beyond it voids the trend signal. Intraday penetration doesn't.
- **Size doesn't matter.** Even a 4-cent window counts. Large windows give a wide zone, so the far edge is the "last gasp" level.
- Aggressive traders may buy near the top of a rising window without waiting for price to enter it, with a mental stop on a close under the window's bottom.
- If one window breaks, look to the **next window below** as the next support.
- **Windows outrank single candles.** A hammer that forms on a falling-window day is still capped by that window, so wait for a close above the window. "Between the window and another pattern, I go with the window."
- On intraday charts, windows usually form between the last bar of one day and the first bar of the next.
- **Three-windows rule (traditional):** after three rising (or falling) windows, the market is overextended. Nison's refinement: **the trend stays intact no matter how many windows there are, until the most recent window is closed** (a close back through it).
- Windows can combine with Western patterns. An **island bottom/top** is a gap one way followed by a gap the other way. The new window gives an *earlier* support or resistance level than the island extreme.
- A **high-volume window** is stronger support or resistance.

**Window-based patterns (the window is what matters; the candle colors after it hardly matter):**
- **Upward-gapping tasuki:** a rising window between two white candles, then a black candle that opens inside the 2nd white body and closes below it, but not below the window. **Downward-gapping tasuki:** the mirror image. Both are rare. Nison: "don't waste brain cells"; just treat the window as support or resistance.
- **High-price gapping play:** after a sharp rally, small bodies consolidate near the highs (relieving overbought), then a rising window opens. Bullish. It is voided by a close under that window.
- **Low-price gapping play:** after a sharp decline, small bodies form a seeming base, then a falling window breaks lower. Bearish.
- **Upgap side-by-side white lines:** a rising window followed by two similar-sized white candles with about the same open. Bullish continuation.
- **Downgap side-by-side white lines:** a falling window followed by two similar white candles (short covering). Still bearish because of the window. Rare.

## 6. Longer-Term Tops and Bottoms (Ch. 6)

- **Three mountain top:** three tries at about the same high (the Western triple top). The peaks need not be exact. Ideally the last peak is confirmed by a bearish candle (doji, dark-cloud cover, engulfing pattern).
- **Three Buddha top:** a three mountain top whose middle peak is highest. It equals the **head-and-shoulders top**, and Japan used it about 100 years before the West. Use the **neckline**: once broken, it becomes resistance. Don't buy bullish candles below a broken neckline. Wait for a close back above it.
- **Three river bottom:** three tests of a low. Confirm with a close above the highs between the troughs.
- **Inverted three Buddha:** equals the inverse head-and-shoulders. The prior resistance becomes support after the breakout.
- **Dumpling top:** a convex/rounded top of mostly small bodies (higher highs, then equal highs, then lower highs), **confirmed by a falling window**, however small.
- **Frypan bottom:** a concave/rounded bottom (lower lows, then equal lows, then higher lows), **confirmed by a rising window**. The market becomes "immune to bad news." It is more significant than a Western rounding bottom because of the gap. The window later acts as support or resistance.
- **Tower top:** at highs, one or more tall white candles, then a lull of small bodies, then one or more tall black candles. **Tower bottom:** the mirror image. These are similar to Western spike/V reversals. They are like dumpling/frypan patterns but need **long candles and no gap**. They often finalize late, so they suit longer-term traders. An earlier warning (such as a harami) may come within the lull.
- **The number three** is important in Japanese technicals (three soldiers, crows, mountains, rivers, methods, three-candle stars). The number four is unlucky.

## 8. Part 2: Convergence ("The Best of East and West")

**Definition:** *convergence* is a cluster of technical signals (candle and/or Western) at or near the same price. The more signals converge at a support or resistance area, the more likely a reversal there.

**Why add Western tools to candles:**
1. Candles are a tool, not a system. A candle signal confirming a Western signal raises the odds.
2. Candles give **no price targets**. Measured moves, prior S/R, retracements, and trendlines provide targets and stop levels.
3. Know what the competition watches, because widely followed Western levels move markets.

### 8.1 Cluster of Candles (Ch. 10)
- Several candle signals at one price level (e.g., an engulfing low, then a long-legged doji, a hammer, a piercing pattern, and a rising window at the same support) magnify that level's importance.
- Resistance example: a shooting star, then a bearish engulfing pattern, then a harami at the same ceiling.
- A row of shooting stars, or of long lower shadows, at one price is a quick visual read of supply or demand.

### 8.2 Trendlines (Ch. 11)
- **Rising support line:** connect 2 or more (preferably 3+) higher reaction lows, **using the bottoms of the lower shadows**. It shows "more aggressive buyers," not "more buyers."
- **Falling support line:** connects lower lows. It gives support when none is otherwise obvious (new lows or all-time lows). Bounces from it tend to be marginal and temporary, but they are buyable if other signals converge there.
- **Falling resistance line:** connects lower highs **using the tops of the upper shadows**.
- **Rising resistance line:** connects higher highs. Use it when the market is at all-time highs and there is no overhead resistance. It gives a supply area as price climbs.
- **Workflow:** get the turning signal from the candles, then use the trendline as the *target* and the opposite extreme as the stop. A non-ideal candle pattern gains credibility if it forms on a trendline.

**Springs and upthrusts (from Richard Wyckoff):**
- **Spring:** the market breaks below horizontal support, then gets back above it (new lows not sustained).
  - Buy when it's back above the old support.
  - **Stop:** under the recent (spring) low, on a close.
  - **Target:** the high before the spring, or the top of the range.
  - More bullish if the break is on light volume and the recovery on heavy volume.
- **Upthrust:** a false breakout above horizontal resistance that falls back under it.
  - Sell on the return below resistance.
  - **Stop:** above the upthrust high.
  - **Target:** the low where the rally started, or the bottom of the range. Use a conservative target and a liberal one when the start point is ambiguous.
  - More bearish if the breakout is on low volume and the return on high volume.
- Either can form within a single session, for example a hammer that is also a spring, or a shooting star that is also an upthrust.
- **"Scouting party" idea:** a breakout must hold (a close beyond the level and staying there) to establish a beachhead. A failure to hold new highs or lows is a signal in itself.
- General rule: **a new high (or low) that can't be sustained has reversal implications.**

**Change of polarity principle:**
- **Old support becomes new resistance, and old resistance becomes new support.**
- Psychology: traders who bought at former support are trapped when it breaks. "The most important price on any chart is the price at which you entered." They sell rallies back to breakeven, which turns old support into resistance.
- The level works best after several tests. It can be a zone rather than an exact price.
- It combines with candles, for example a hammer on the retest of a broken resistance line, or a dark-cloud cover at a broken-support zone.

### 8.3 Retracements (Ch. 12)
- Popular retracement levels are **38.2%, 50%, and 61.8%** (Fibonacci). 50% is the most widely watched (Gann, Elliott, Dow).
- **Rule:** don't buy or sell just because price reached a retracement level. **Wait for a confirming candle signal there** (for example a piercing pattern at 50%, or a high-wave candle at 38.2%).
- These levels are strongest when they coincide with change-of-polarity zones or clusters of long lower shadows.

### 8.4 Moving Averages (Ch. 13)
- **Types:**
  - **Simple:** the equal-weighted average of N prices.
  - **Weighted:** front-loaded weights.
  - **Exponential:** includes all past data with exponentially decreasing weights. It is used in MACD.
- **Length:** shorter averages hug price more closely and give more whipsaws. Longer averages are smoother but lag.
- **Popular lengths:** 5, 9, 30, 50, and 200-day. Nison uses the **5 and 30-day for equities** and the **40 and 65-day for futures**. The 9-period EMA is popular in FX. These are not "optimized" values.
- **Uses:**
  1. **Trend filter:** price above the MA, e.g. above the 65-day for an intermediate uptrend, or above the 40-week for a long-term uptrend.
  2. **Support/resistance:** a close above is bullish and a close below is bearish.
  3. **Envelopes/bands:** a percentage above or below the MA acts as S/R.
  4. **Slope:** flattening or turning down after a rise is bearish. Trendlines can be drawn on the MA itself.
  5. **Dual MAs:** a short MA crossing under a long MA is a **"dead cross"** (bearish), and crossing over is a **"golden cross"** (bullish). Stricter users also require both MAs to slope in the trade direction.
- **Key rule:** first verify that the MA has been respected recently. Then **act only when a candle signal confirms the MA's support or resistance**. Never trade on the MA alone.
- A **cluster of bullish candles under MA resistance is not a buy until there is a close above the MA**.
- An MA that was good support usually becomes good resistance once broken, and vice versa.

### 8.5 Oscillators (Ch. 14)
- **Three uses:**
  1. **Divergence:** a price high not confirmed by the oscillator is bearish, and a price low not confirmed is bullish.
  2. **Overbought/oversold.**
  3. **Momentum confirmation:** flattening momentum warns that the move is decelerating.
- **Nison's metaphor:** "All clouds do not rain." An overbought or oversold oscillator is only a *cloud* (warning), and **the candle signal is the raindrop (confirmation)**. Also, overbought or oversold readings help define the "high-price/low-price area" that many candle patterns require.
- **RSI:**
  - RSI = 100 − 100/(1+RS), where RS = average up-close points ÷ average down-close points over N periods. 9 and 14 periods are common, and it uses closes only.
  - Above 70 is overbought and below 30 is oversold.
  - Divergence is more meaningful when it occurs in the extreme zones.
  - Trendlines can be drawn on the RSI itself.
  - Examples: a dark-cloud cover plus RSI overbought plus bearish divergence is a strong top. A bullish engulfing pattern *with* bullish divergence beats one without it.
- **Moving-average oscillator:**
  - It is the difference between a short and a long MA, oscillating around 0.
  - A wide gap means overbought or oversold.
  - A narrowing gap while price rises means short-term momentum is fading.
  - It is also used for divergence.
  - **Exit tool for longs:** an overbought oscillator plus a confirmed candle top (hanging man confirmed, doji, bearish engulfing) is the place to exit or lighten. An overbought reading *without* a candle signal, or with a rising window still holding as support, is **not** a sell.
- **Stochastics:**
  - Raw %K = (Close − Low_N) / (High_N − Low_N) × 100, with 14, 19, or 21 periods common.
  - Slow %K is the 3-period average of raw %K. %D is the 3-period average of slow %K.
  - Above 80 is overbought and below 20 is oversold.
  - Signals are divergence and %K/%D crossovers. A strict buy needs an oversold %D, plus positive divergence, plus %K crossing above %D.
  - A small or imperfect candle pattern (such as a bearish engulfing pattern with equal-sized bodies) gains weight when it comes with a stochastic crossover in an extreme zone.
- **MACD:**
  - The MACD line is the difference between the 12 and 26-period EMAs. The signal line is the 9-period EMA of the MACD line.
  - The fast line crossing above the signal line is bullish, and crossing below is bearish.
  - It is slower, so it is not mainly a short-term tool.
  - **Candles often turn days before the MACD crossover.** Use the MACD as later confirmation (for example a dark-cloud cover about a week before the MACD cross, or an evening star followed by a MACD bearish cross).

### 8.6 Volume (Ch. 15)
- Volume is like water pressure in a hose: more volume means more force behind the move.
- Volume should **expand in the direction of the trend**. Shrinking volume makes continuation less likely.
- Read volume **relatively** (spikes versus recent sessions), not in absolute numbers. Volume-width candles are optional, and a plain volume histogram is fine.
- **Volume and candles:**
  - **High-volume hammer:** more reliable and less likely to retest its low.
  - **Reversal patterns:** light volume on the first (old-trend) candle and heavy volume on the second (new-trend) candle. This applies to engulfing patterns, piercing patterns, dark-cloud covers, and stars (light on candle 1, heavy on candle 3).
  - **High-volume spinning top or doji after a steep decline:** heavy supply is being absorbed by equally aggressive demand, a possible bottom (stronger than a normal Southern doji).
  - **High-volume spinning top or doji after an extended advance:** buying is meeting enough supply to stall, a possible top.
  - **High-volume window:** stronger support or resistance.
  - **Rising three methods:** heavy volume on the long candles and light volume on the pause.
  - **Low-volume breakout to new highs (even with a tall white candle):** be more circumspect, because it is less likely to be sustained.
  - Heavy opening volume on a dark-cloud cover's gap-up suggests a buying blow-off.
- Upside breakouts need volume confirmation more than downside ones, since markets can fall of their own weight.

### 8.7 Measured Moves / Price Targets (Ch. 16)
- **Purpose:** a target is for **exiting an existing position, not initiating a new one**. A target is not automatically support or resistance, and price may overshoot it (e.g., NASDAQ went through its 2750 target). Fade a target only if a candle reversal or other convergence appears there.
- **Box range breakout:**
  - The market is in a range ("box," a state of "wa" or harmony), like a coiled spring.
  - **Target = box height added to the breakout level** (or subtracted on a downside break). Example: a $50-53 box gives $56 up or $47 down.
  - The broken box edge then flips polarity (old resistance becomes support), and a close back inside **voids the target**.
- **Swing target:** a sharp leg A→B, then a correction to C. The target is C + (B − A).
- **Flags and pennants:**
  - A sharp move (the "flagpole") is followed by a brief consolidation. A flag is a horizontal box, and a pennant has converging highs and lows.
  - **Nison's conservative target:** flagpole height added to the **bottom** of a bull flag/pennant, or subtracted from the **top** of a bear flag/pennant. The classic method uses the other edge. He prefers exiting slightly early to missing the move.
  - The rising three methods is the candle cousin of these patterns.
- **Ascending triangle:** flat resistance with rising lows. On a breakout, **target = triangle height at its widest point added to the broken resistance**.
- **Descending triangle:** flat support with falling highs. On a breakdown, **target = widest height subtracted from the broken support**. The broken support then becomes resistance, and a falling window there adds convergence.

### 8.8 Full Convergence Case Study (Ch. 17, NASDAQ top, 2000)
Signals that converged in the 5050-5150 area:
1. A rising resistance line drawn through the highs, which gave a supply area in uncharted territory.
2. A daily shooting star at that line.
3. An hourly bearish engulfing pattern on the same day.
4. Bearish oscillator divergence (a new price high with a lower oscillator high).
5. A small falling window that turned the trend down and became resistance.
6. The rebound stalled at the window with a high-wave candle that failed to close above it.

- **Lesson:** a rare abundance of East and West signals at one level greatly raised the odds of a major top. **Still, none of it forecast how far the decline would go.**
- Using a lower timeframe (hourly) to confirm a daily signal is part of convergence.

## 9. Trading, Risk, and Discipline Tips

- **Protective stops:**
  - Set the stop **when you place the trade**, because that is when you are most objective.
  - Stay in only while the market behaves as expected.
  - "Lose your opinion, not your money." "The one thing worse than being wrong is staying wrong."
  - All long-term trends start as short-term moves, so don't dismiss an adverse move.
  - **There is no room for hope** in trading. Think of a stop as a cost of doing business, or "a hook well lost to catch a salmon."
  - Capital preservation is rule #1 (Buffett's two rules).
  - The general's analogy: make tactical retreats to preserve troops (capital) and equanimity.
- **DISCIPLINE acronym (Nison's rules):**
  - **D**on't forget old support and resistance (polarity).
  - **I**f/then: if the market behaves as anticipated, stay. Otherwise exit.
  - **S**tops: always use them.
  - **C**onsider options.
  - **I**ntraday technicals matter even for non-day-traders.
  - **P**ace trades to the market environment (adapt your style to conditions).
  - **L**ocals: never forget them.
  - **I**ndicators: the more the better (convergence).
  - **N**ever trade in the belief the market is wrong.
  - **E**xamine the market's reaction to news.
- **"The market is never wrong":** don't impose your view (the steamroller analogy). Be a **trend follower, not a trend predictor**. Waiting for the right moment is a virtue.
- **Reaction to news:** if bullish news fails to lift price (or a frypan bottom forms despite bad news), that tells you a lot. "What matters is not the events but the human reaction to them" (Baruch).
- **Technicals foster objectivity:** they define entries, exits, reward-to-risk, and stop levels, which counters the urge to "root for your position." The emotional impact grows with the size of the position, so paper trading differs from real trading.
- **Practical rules repeated throughout the book:**
  - Wait for the **close** to validate patterns and breakouts. Use market-on-close orders or anticipate the close, or wait for the next open.
  - **Don't buy a bullish pattern at resistance or sell a bearish one at support.** Wait for a close through the level.
  - **When a pattern completes far from its extreme, wait for a pullback** to the pattern's support or resistance (engulfing low/high, star extreme, hammer shadow, dark-cloud high, first or second soldier).
  - Use reversal signals in the direction of the **major trend** for new positions. Countertrend signals are for exits.
  - Treat a single candle (doji, harami, spinning top) as a reason to **adjust**: scale back, tighten stops, or sell covered calls. Don't flip the position.
  - **Adapt quickly:** a breakout followed the next day by a dark-cloud cover or harami changes the picture. Act as a "detective" collecting small clues ("the water of a great ocean comes one drop at a time").
  - **Separate signals from targets:** candles provide entry timing, and Western measured moves, trendlines, and prior S/R provide exits.
  - Specialists use candles for reversals and other methods (e.g., point & figure) for targets.
  - "Don't climb a tree to catch a fish": don't expect candles to forecast the size of a move.
- **Final word (Conclusion):** a bullish candle in a major bear market is not a buy. A bullish candle confirmed by other signals in a bull market is. Weigh reward-to-risk on every signal. How you trade candles depends on your philosophy, risk tolerance, and temperament.

## 10. Glossary Highlights (terms not covered above)

- **Box range:** a lateral trading range. **Wa:** the harmony or equilibrium of a box.
- **Raindrop:** a star in a downtrend.
- **Power line:** a white hammer.
- **Hugging line:** an engulfing pattern.
- **Petrifying pattern:** a harami cross.
- **Rickshaw man:** a long-legged doji with the open/close mid-range.
- **Golden cross / dead cross:** short MA crossing above / below the long MA.
- **Western terms:**
  - **Reversal session (outside reversal):** a new high for the move, then a close under the prior close.
  - **Inside session:** the whole range is inside the prior range.
  - **Island:** gaps on both sides of one or more sessions.
  - **Blow-off / selling climax:** a sharp final thrust (often on heavy volume) followed by a reversal.
  - **Breakaway gap:** a gap away from a significant technical area.
