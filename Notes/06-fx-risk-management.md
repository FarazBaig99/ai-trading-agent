# 06 – Foreign Exchange Risk Management

**Source:** Isle of Man Financial Services Authority, *Guidance Note for Deposit Takers (Class 1(1) and Class 1(2)): Foreign Exchange Risk Management*, Version 1.1, March 2017 (13 pp.). Read in full.

**What it is:** a regulator's best-practice guidance for banks on how to identify, measure, limit and report FX risk. It is guidance, not law, but following it suggests compliance. Part 1 covers Isle of Man–incorporated banks. Part 2 covers branches of foreign banks and largely repeats Part 1 in shorter form. The document contains **no market research or trading strategy**. Its value for a trader is the risk-control framework, which scales down to a personal account (see section 8).

---

## 1. Why FX risk management matters

- The FX market is arguably the **largest and most liquid** market. Large, fast moves are normal.
- FX risk does not only come from deliberate trading positions. It also comes from:
  - client-driven deals;
  - **known future profit flows** in foreign currency;
  - **bad-debt provisions** in foreign currency.
- All such exposures should be identified and, where needed, **hedged promptly**.

## 2. Risk types (the five categories)

| Risk | Definition | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **Exchange rate risk (open position)** | Loss from adverse FX moves while holding an open position | Open position = assets/inflows in a currency do not equal liabilities/outflows. **Long** (assets > liabilities) loses if the currency **falls**. **Short** (liabilities > assets) loses if the currency **rises**. |
| **Interest rate risk** | Arises from **unmatched forward** FX deals | A change in either currency's interest rate changes the forward's value |
| **Settlement risk** (time-zone / **Herstatt risk**) | Credit risk because the two currencies settle in different time zones | Example: selling AUD for USD means paying AUD about **15 hours** before receiving USD. A deal is complete only when the last time zone settles. |
| **Credit risk** | Counterparty fails or defaults | If it fails **before** any settlement, the loss is limited to the gap between the contract rate and the current market rate (in effect, exchange rate risk). If it fails **after one side has settled**, you can lose the full principal. |
| **Country risk** | Credit risk tied to the currency or the counterparty's country, not the counterparty itself | Sanctions or political events (e.g. UN or local government) can stop you selling balances or delivering currency on completed trades. Relevant for emerging markets. |

- The **most common** risk is exchange rate risk. It comes from two places:
  1. FX deals with clients and counterparties, including proprietary trading.
  2. P&L effects of foreign-currency deposit/loan books and operating cash flows.

### Notes on each
- **Open-position risk from client business** is easy to measure, provided deals are **booked immediately** and dealers are told about large deals.
- **FX risk embedded in the P&L** (foreign-currency profit streams) is harder to measure. Banks with material foreign profit flows should formally assess whether to hedge. The hedge or no-hedge decision should be **minuted** at Management or **ALCO** (Asset and Liability Committee).
- **Interest rate risk example:**
  - Buy GBP spot vs USD, and sell the same GBP 3 months forward.
  - The pure FX exposure is only the **difference between the spot and forward USD amounts**.
  - The bank still holds a **long GBP / short USD forward** position, which gains or loses as GBP or USD interest rates move.
  - Rates are less volatile than FX, but the risk must still be measured.
- **Settlement, credit and country risk** arise mainly when physical cash settles. They are managed through **credit limits** approved by the Board on Treasury/ALCO advice.

## 3. The regulator's quantitative expectations

- A **Board-approved FX risk policy** plus systems that measure, monitor and enforce it, scaled to the bank's size and complexity.
- **10/10 rule:** no specific limits are imposed, but a **10% adverse FX move** should not cost more than **10% of the bank's large exposures capital base (LECB)**.
- **Capital:** a **100% weighting** applies to the net reportable open position.
- **Stress testing:** at least **every six months**, assess the cost of a **20% shock** move in exchange rates.

## 4. Contents of an FX risk management policy

The policy should define permitted instruments, limits suited to the business, reporting frequency, and who reports. It should be **reviewed at least annually**. Checklist:

1. **Open-position limits** in line with client turnover and risk appetite.
2. **Per-currency limits plus an overall cap.** Proprietary traders also need **intraday limits**, normally at most **5× the overnight cap**.
3. **Settlement and country limits** when trading with non-group counterparties.
4. **Forward FX mismatch (gap) limits.**
5. **Approved instrument list.**
6. Rules on **FX derivatives**.
7. **Expertise and experience** required of authorised staff.
8. Authority to trade with non-group counterparties.
9. **Monitoring and reporting systems.**
10. **Recording and follow-up of limit breaches.**
11. **P&L impact of a 10% adverse move** on the maximum permitted exposure.
12. A **stop-loss limit**: once hit, no new trading except client deals and hedges.
13. **Segregation of duties**: front office separate from back office and valuation.
14. **Trading mandates** for each authorised person.
15. Limits on **out-of-hours trading**.
16. List of **authorised brokers**.
17. **Code of conduct** for dealers.

## 5. Procedures and systems

- Banks taking FX risk should be able to **measure positions continuously** and **report to management daily**. This requires:
  - clear responsibility for measuring and reporting;
  - reliable systems that produce accurate reports quickly;
  - active senior management involvement;
  - regular reporting to group or parent (for branches, this may be done at head office).
- The risk system should **link to core banking systems** and be **reconcilable** to core data.
- **Good management information:**
  - clarity;
  - highlight key information, **especially breaches and exceptions**;
  - show **unused limit capacity**;
  - **exception-based** commentary.

## 6. Net open position (NOP) calculation

- Calculate the NOP **for each currency, including gold**, but **excluding the base currency**.
- **Base currency** = the currency of most of the bank's capital and of its statutory accounts, normally GBP. Using another base currency needs regulator permission, and the GBP NOP must then also be calculated.
- **Formula:**

  **Net open position (per currency) = Net spot position + Net forward position**

  Detailed rules are in chapter 11 of the regulator's prudential-returns guidance.
  - Conceptually: net spot = spot assets − spot liabilities in that currency; net forward = forward purchases − forward sales.
  - Positive = long; negative = short.

## 7. Valuation rules

- Calculate the net position **at least weekly**.
- Book **revaluation P&L** on residual positions **at least monthly**.
- Use **closing mid-market rates**, ideally sourced or at least **independently checked by non-dealing staff**. Convert to base currency at closing mid spot.
- Revalue **forwards** at the prevailing **outright mid forward rate** for the remaining period.
- **Mark to market** other FX contracts.

## 8. Hedging and control methods referred to (summary)

- **Natural matching:** balance assets and liabilities per currency so the open position is near zero.
- **Forward contracts:** lock in rates for known foreign-currency flows (profits, provisions). Watch the interest-rate and mismatch risk they create.
- **FX derivatives:** allowed if listed in the policy.
- **Limits as a hedge against yourself:** per-currency, overall, intraday, forward-gap, counterparty, settlement and country limits.
- **Stop-loss limit:** a hard halt on speculative trading after a set loss.
- **Stress testing** (20% shock) and **sensitivity** analysis (10% move vs capital).
- **Governance:** Board-approved policy, minuted hedge decisions, segregation of duties, independent rate checks, exception reporting.

## 9. Practical takeaways for a short-term trader (adapting the bank framework)

1. **Know your open position at all times.** Long a currency means you lose if it falls; short means you lose if it rises. Several pairs sharing a currency (e.g. long EUR/USD and long GBP/USD) are **one large short-USD position**. Add exposure up by currency, not by trade.
2. **Use a 10/10-style sizing rule.** Size so that a plausible adverse move costs only a small, pre-set fraction of capital. The bank standard is 10% move → ≤10% of capital. For a retail trader, a common equivalent is ≤1–2% of the account per trade.
3. **Set limits per currency and overall, plus an intraday cap.** Write them down before you trade.
4. **Daily stop-loss: stop trading when the loss limit is hit.** The bank rule "only client deals and hedges after the stop-loss" becomes, for an individual, "no new speculative trades for the rest of the day/week".
5. **Stress-test regularly.** Ask what happens to the account in a 20% shock or a weekend gap (e.g. a central bank surprise or a de-peg). If the answer is a wipe-out, size down.
6. **Mark to market honestly with independent prices.** Do not judge P&L on hope; use closing mid prices.
7. **Keep a breach log and review it.** Record every limit breach and why it happened. This is the retail version of "recording and follow-up of limit excesses".
8. **Counterparty, settlement and country risk apply to your broker.** Your broker is your counterparty. Check its regulation, how client funds are segregated, and its jurisdiction. Know that sanctions or capital controls can freeze currencies or withdrawals.
9. **Avoid trading during illiquid out-of-hours periods** unless you have a specific reason. The regulator expects banks to restrict this.
10. **Hold yourself to a code of conduct and a trading mandate:** a written plan covering allowed instruments, maximum size and allowed hours.
