## 1. Why fundamentals matter, even on a 5-minute chart

- Forex looks like a game but is **not a fair game of chance**. Participants do **not** have equal information.
  - Fundamental information reaches people at different times (**information asymmetry**), and that creates edges.
- **You cannot separate fundamentals from technicals** without distorting your picture of the market and taking avoidable losses.
  - **Key claim: a 5-minute trader who trades on the side of the longer-term trend is more likely to be profitable.**
- Every economic transaction settles in some currency. Trading FX makes you a participant in the global economy, riding a "global wave" of **economic, geopolitical and emotional** influences.
- The first step to finding high-probability trades is understanding **what moves currencies over time**.
- Traders should follow the **central bank heads** daily, because their words and decisions drive rate expectations and USD sentiment. The 2014–15 list:
  - Yellen (Fed);
  - Draghi (ECB);
  - Kuroda (BoJ);
  - Carney (BoE);
  - Stevens (RBA);
  - Poloz (BoC);
  - Jordan (SNB);
  - Zhou Xiaochuan (PBoC).
- Geopolitical flashpoints (Baghdad, Tehran, Crimea, Gaza) trigger fear. Corporate news (e.g. Wal-Mart sales) shifts views on US growth.
- Together these shift sentiment about growth in the US, Eurozone, UK, China and Japan. Sentiment shifts move the buyer/seller balance and therefore price.

## 2. The main ingredient: interest rates and rate expectations

- Interest rates are the "dough" of the fundamental pie. They are the main tool central banks use to accelerate or brake their economies.
- **History:**
  - **ECB:** raised rates **8 times** (December 2005 to June 2007) to 4.0% to cool a booming economy and inflation.
  - **Fed:** raised rates **17 times** (June 2004 to August 2006), then paused.
  - **2008 crisis:** described as a "12 standard deviation" event that nobody foresaw. It led to near-**zero rates** worldwide. With rate cuts exhausted, central banks used **quantitative easing (QE)**, i.e. expanding the money supply to stimulate demand.
  - **Late 2014:**
    - the Fed **ended QE (October 2014)** and put rate hikes back on the agenda;
    - the BoE signalled hikes;
    - New Zealand was the only Western economy actually raising rates;
    - the Eurozone feared **deflation** and kept rates low;
    - the BoJ continued QE, targeting **2% inflation**.
- **The core message:** what matters is the **expectation** of rate changes (staying low vs rising), not just the current level.

### Inflation: two kinds for the FX trader
1. **Demand inflation:** rising consumer spending pushes prices up.
2. **Wage inflation:** a shortage of workers pushes wages up. Central banks focus on it more and more.
- **Puzzle (2014–15):** wage-inflation **expectations** were rising, but little actual wage inflation appeared. The world was shifting to lower growth and lower inflation.
  - The result is constant focus on data releases, so **more surprise potential and more trading opportunity**.

### Why rate hikes strengthen a currency
- Higher rates slow the economy, and they also **attract capital** into bonds and other interest-bearing assets. This is the "**appetite for yield**".
- Globally, **interest-rate differentials** drive cross-border capital flows.
- **Non-uniform** hikes across countries mean divergence, which means trading opportunities "not seen in a decade or more".

### The carry trade
- Definition: **borrow (sell) a low-yield currency and invest in (buy) a high-yield currency** to earn the rate differential.
- Example: Japan 0.10% vs New Zealand 3.5%, i.e. borrow yen and buy kiwi. The yen has historically been the main funding currency.
- **Risk: carry unwinds cause turbulence.**
  - **27 February 2007:** carry positions were unwound. Billions were converted back into yen, and equities were sold to fund the yen buy-backs. USD/JPY slid and the Dow sold off with it.
- **Risk-on / risk-off rule:**
  - **USD/JPY tends to correlate positively with US equities** (Dow).
  - **Risk-averse:** the yen strengthens (USD/JPY falls).
  - **Risk-on:** the yen weakens (USD/JPY rises).
  - Figure 1.2: from 2011 to 2014, USD/JPY and the Dow rose together.

## 3. Housing data as a driver of currencies

- **Why housing:**
  - it is very **sensitive to interest rates**;
  - it is a major source of household **wealth, consumer spending and jobs**.
  - Watch especially for **housing bubbles**.
- **Pre-2008 global boom:** house prices rose >10% a year in many countries.
  - Ireland +15% (2006); Spain +13%; Canada, Norway and Sweden >10%; US +7%.
  - The wealth effect fuelled growth and consumption.
- **Key housing releases to track:**
  - unsold homes (inventory);
  - mortgage loan applications;
  - new and existing home sales;
  - single-family housing permits;
  - housing prices.
- **Transmission chain:**
  - weak housing → consumers feel poorer and cut spending → expected slowdown → **lower odds of rate hikes** → currency-bearish;
  - the reverse is currency-bullish.

### Mortgage equity withdrawal (MEW)
- In a boom, homeowners borrow against rising home equity, which boosts consumption and GDP. MEWs rise in housing booms.
- A **slowdown in MEW** signals weaker consumption and a **lower likelihood of rate hikes**.
- Research cited (Carroll et al., via the NY Times, 2006):
  - **Each $1,000 change in housing wealth changes immediate consumption by about $20.**
  - The **housing wealth effect is about twice the stock-market wealth effect**.
  - Economists remain unsure exactly how MEW cash is used: paying down card debt, renovation, saving, buying more homes.
- **2008:** falling house values wiped out equity, so MEW collapsed.
  - **Subprime** loans with very high loan-to-value ratios amplified the collapse through delinquencies.
  - The BoE no longer publishes MEW separately, but traders should watch for MEW uptrends.
- **Rule:** stronger housing → stronger consumer demand → higher odds of rate hikes → **bullish currency**. It is hard to be bullish on a currency whose housing sector is weak.

### Housing as a leading indicator
- Economies do not turn overnight. Housing data let you form a bullish or bearish **fundamental bias before technical patterns show the turn**.
- **US housing starts example:**
  - 2005 was very strong, peaking at about **2.2 million units** (annualised) in early 2005.
  - January 2006 retested the peak; by August 2006 starts were back at 2003 levels.
  - After the peak it was hard to be pro-dollar.
  - The table shows (presumably monthly) starts falling from 197,900 (May 2005) to 44,500 (October 2009), then 84,400 by October 2014, still **not fully recovered**.
- **Generalisation:** when a country's housing data trend up, its currency **attracts capital**, mainly because **rate-hike expectations** build.

### Housing sentiment indicators (more timely than lagging hard data)
- **NAHB Housing Market Index (HMI):** a monthly builder survey (now NAHB/Wells Fargo). **Above 50 = most builders see good conditions.**
- **S&P/Case-Shiller Home Price Index:** benchmark US single-family prices across **20 metro areas**, monthly, released on the **last Tuesday of the month at 9 a.m.** (ET).
  - In late 2014 it was trending up into new recovery territory but **still below the 2006 peak**.

### Housing equities as a real-time proxy
- Watch homebuilder stocks and housing ETFs, e.g. **Lennar (LEN)**.
  - Lennar began falling in **early 2005**, an early omen that **Fed rate hikes were ending**.
  - In early 2007 its CEO said there was no sign of a bottom and announced $400–500 million in land write-downs.
- **Assignment idea:** track the top housing stocks and ETFs weekly. When they test weekly **support, resistance or trendlines**, a housing turn (and a rate-expectation shift) may be coming.

## 4. Housing in other countries: focus on the UK

- **Shortcut:** read **central bank reports** and search news (Google, Baidu, Yahoo) for local housing conditions.
- UK housing directly drives **BoE** rate decisions and therefore **GBP**.
- **2014 UK data:**
  - The Telegraph: first-time buyers kept "piling into a hot market" despite high prices and looming hikes.
  - **Council of Mortgage Lenders (CML), June 2014:** 28,600 first-time-buyer loans, the highest since December 2007 (+7.1% m/m, +18.7% y/y). Amount lent £4.2bn (vs £3.8bn in May and £3.3bn a year earlier), the highest since September 2007.
  - **BoE Financial Stability Report (June 2014):**
    - prices still rising and price expectations strong;
    - the share of high **loan-to-income** mortgages was increasing, a risk if incomes or rates are shocked.
    - So the BoE faced a **balancing act**: hiking curbs inflation but stresses indebted households.
  - **Structural shortage:** net new private housing supply was 110,000 in 2013 vs a 2007 average of 180,000, so UK housing pressure persists.
- **GBP/USD traders gain an edge** by watching UK housing data for clues on whether cable will rise.

## 5. Chapter summary (author's)

- Tracking whether an economy is strengthening or weakening is central to calling a currency's direction, everywhere in the world.
- The main fundamental focus is **interest rates**: **rate increases tend to strengthen a currency**.
- Look beyond current rate levels to **whether the economy is expected to strengthen or weaken**.
- Track **housing data**, because it feeds central-bank decisions to raise, cut or hold rates.

## 7. Practical takeaways for a short-term trader

1. **Set a fundamental bias first, then trade technicals in that direction.** The author's central point: short-timeframe (even 5-minute) trades aligned with the longer-term, fundamentally driven trend have better odds.
2. **Rate expectations are the master variable.** Know which central banks are in a hiking, holding or cutting phase. Trade the **divergence** (long the currency of the hawkish bank, short the dovish one).
3. **Watch central bankers' speeches and data surprises.** In low-growth, low-inflation regimes, markets react strongly to surprises in inflation, wages and housing.
4. **Use USD/JPY as a risk barometer.** It tends to move with equities: a falling USD/JPY with falling stocks means risk-off. Beware carry-trade unwinds, which can be sudden and sharp (e.g. 27 February 2007).
5. **Treat housing as a leading signal for rates and currency.** Rising starts, permits, NAHB >50, rising Case-Shiller and strong homebuilder stocks point to rate-hike odds and support the currency; the reverse is bearish. Put these release times on your calendar (e.g. Case-Shiller on the last Tuesday, 9 a.m. ET) and avoid opening very short-expiry trades right into them.
6. **Use cross-market tells.** Homebuilder stock breakdowns can precede a shift in rate expectations. Equity-index direction can foreshadow yen moves.
7. **For GBP trades, follow UK housing and BoE financial-stability commentary.** BoE caution about household debt can temper hike expectations even when housing is hot.
8. **Information asymmetry is real.** Retail traders get news last, so do not chase the first spike after a release. Wait for the market to digest it and trade the resulting structure.
